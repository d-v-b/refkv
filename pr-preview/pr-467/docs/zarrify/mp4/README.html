MP4¶

refkv turns a file into a Zarr dataset by writing a small archive of references to the file’s bytes, without copying its content (overview). This page is about MP4 and QuickTime files (.mp4, .m4a, .mov): video and audio, which, like MP3, are not images.

Who this is for¶

H.264 in MP4 is how most video is recorded and shared, scientific video included: behavior recordings from lab cameras (the input of pose estimation), microscope and field recordings, the external video files an NWB file points to. HEVC is what phones record: an iPhone’s .MOV files are HEVC by default, 10-bit HDR on recent models. Someone who wants to read a few seconds of a long recording as a NumPy array, over HTTP, without decoding the whole file or converting it to images first, has to write that machinery today.

An MP4 file indexes every coded frame (its offset, size, timestamp and whether it is a keyframe) in its moov box, and a fragmented one, as a camera or a live stream records it, in a moof box per fragment, so refkv reads those boxes alone, not the media, and writes an archive in which every Zarr chunk is a run of frames, referenced together with the frames before it that a decoder needs: back to the keyframe before it, for video. The archive’s refkv.stream codec decodes a chunk when it is read.

What you get¶

A group at the archive root, and a group per track refkv zarrifies, at tracks/<id>.

  • Video (H.264 and AV1 4:2:0 of 8 or 10 bits; VP9 4:2:0 or 4:2:2 of 8, 10 or 12; HEVC 4:0:0, 4:2:0 or 4:2:2 of 8, 10 or 12): three arrays, y, u and v, the decoder’s planes, [time, y, x] (the chroma planes half the size along each axis, or along x alone for 4:2:2; y alone for monochrome 4:0:0 HEVC), in presentation order: uint8 for 8 bits, uint16 for more (values from 0 to 2^bits − 1); and time, each picture’s presentation time in the track’s timescale (int64). Each chunk is whole groups of pictures from a keyframe, up to 256 pictures and at most 32 MiB of luma (16 pictures at 1080p, 8 of 10 bits); only a group longer than that is split. Chunks of different lengths use the zarr-extensions rectilinear chunk grid, which refkv.zarr turns on in zarr-python. No color conversion: RGB depends on the stream’s color matrix, which is the reader’s to apply.

  • Motion JPEG: pictures, uint8, [time, y, x, c] in RGB ([time, y, x] for a gray stream), the JPEG decoder’s output, a picture a chunk, in presentation order, and time as above.

  • Audio (AAC-LC, HE-AAC, MP3): samples, float32, [time, channel], the decoder’s output, in chunks of 256 frames.

  • Audio (PCM): samples, [time, channel], in the file’s own data type (uint8, int8, int16, int32, float32 or float64) and byte order, in chunks of 262144 frames read with the bytes codec, so no decoder is needed.

  • Codecs: refkv.stream (zarr finds it through refkv’s entry points), which decodes with ffmpeg’s decoders through PyAV (the media extra); imagecodecs_jpeg for Motion JPEG, through imagecodecs (the images extra).

  • Metadata: under refkv_zarrified.mp4 on the root, the brands, the movie’s timescale and duration, and every track: its codec, its path, or why it is not zarrified (mp2v video is not supported: only H.264 (avc1), HEVC (hvc1, hev1), AV1 (av01), VP9 (vp09) and Motion JPEG (jpeg, mjpa, dmb1) are). Each track’s group holds its timescale, edit list, dimensions, bit depth, profile and level, or its sample rate and channel count. The whole file (every box, field by field, every sample, and the media data in no sample) is described on refkv_source by the Rust core’s IR mirror, from which it is rebuilt byte for byte.

What is not expressed. The edit list, which places each track on the movie’s timeline (delaying it, or cutting the encoder’s priming), is recorded, not applied: the arrays hold every decoded picture and sample. So is a phone’s rotation (the track’s matrix: a portrait video is coded sideways) and an HDR video’s transfer function (an iPhone’s is HLG, with Dolby Vision metadata the planes do not apply). AV1, 10-bit H.264 and other codecs’ tracks are listed with the reason and kept in the mirror, as are udta and meta boxes (titles, tags, an iPhone’s location). spec/zarrify/mp4.md §7 lists them.

Try it¶

example.py zarrifies a public file and plots it with matplotlib. Its dependencies are in its header (PEP 723), so it runs on its own, with refkv from PyPI:

uv run docs/zarrify/mp4/example.py

About Bananas (1935), from the Prelinger Archives on the Internet Archive (public domain, 46 MB), from corpus_mp4.txt:

uv run refkv zarrify https://archive.org/download/AboutBan1935/AboutBan1935_512kb.mp4 bananas.vzip

{"format": "mp4", "brand": "isom", "tracks": [{"id": 1, "format": "h264", "path": "tracks/1", "frames": 19901, "chunkFrames": 256, "chunks": 80}, {"id": 2, "format": "aac", "path": "tracks/2", "frames": 28567, "chunkFrames": 256, "chunks": 112}], "skipped": [], ...}

It takes under 4 seconds, reading the moov box alone; the archive is 2.4 MB. Read a picture and its time:

import zarr
import refkv.zarr

g = zarr.open_group(refkv.zarr.open("bananas.vzip"), mode="r")
video = g["tracks/1"]
m = video.attrs["refkv_zarrified"]["mp4"]
print({k: m[k] for k in ("width", "height", "frames", "timescale", "sync_samples")})
y = video["y"][5000]
print(y.shape, y.dtype, video["time"][5000] / m["timescale"])
print(g["tracks/2/samples"].shape)

{'width': 320, 'height': 240, 'frames': 19901, 'timescale': 2997, 'sync_samples': 1660}
(240, 320) uint8 166.8335001668335
(29252608, 2)

Picture 5000 is ffmpeg’s decoding of it, value for value. Reading it fetches and decodes its chunk, 21 groups of 12 pictures from a keyframe, in about 2 seconds over HTTP.

In the browser. The service worker serves a zarrified MP4 as a Zarr hierarchy like any other, and the demo page opens each HEVC track’s and each 8-bit H.264, AV1 and VP9 track’s luma (its Y plane) in Neuroglancer, scrolled over time: the Neuroglancer fork decodes HEVC chunks with libde265 built to wasm, and the others with the browser’s WebCodecs VideoDecoder. The demo’s MP4 examples are behavior videos, read from the DANDI archive’s S3 bucket, which serves its files with CORS (both CC BY 4.0): from DANDI 001195, a study of loneliness-like state in mice, a mouse after 24 hours alone; and from DANDI 001538, a study of levodopa-induced dyskinesia, a parkinsonian mouse 80 minutes after L-DOPA, a QuickTime .mov from Apple’s encoder.

HEVC decodes the same in every browser, Firefox and Linux included, in software: libde265 gives every plane of 4:0:0, 4:2:0 and 4:2:2 pictures of 8 to 12 bits as ffmpeg’s decoding does, sample for sample, on refkv’s fixtures. WebCodecs is not used for HEVC because, measured against ffmpeg’s decoding on macOS with Playwright’s builds, no browser gives every stream’s pictures unconverted. Chrome (Chromium 148) narrows a full-range stream and keeps pictures of more than 8 bits in GPU memory with no pixel format, which WebCodecs cannot copy; headless, it decodes no HEVC. WebKit 26 widens a limited-range stream, gives 10- and 12-bit pictures as 8-bit ones, and its chroma of x265’s streams is off by up to 58 levels with nothing to detect it by. Firefox 150 decodes no HEVC.

The demo opens HEVC tracks of any bit depth, and names a 10-bit H.264, AV1 or VP9 track in its note instead of opening it, since only Chrome gives those pictures unconverted. In Python, PyAV’s software decoder decodes every HEVC track the profile zarrifies, exactly.

Supported and not supported¶

Supported:

  • MP4 and QuickTime files with the moov box before or after the media, 64-bit box sizes and a media box that runs to the end of the file.

  • H.264 (avc1) video in any profile that is 8-bit 4:2:0, with or without B-frames, with open or closed groups of pictures (a camera’s B-frames presented before each keyframe included), at a constant or variable frame rate, any number of tracks.

  • HEVC (hvc1, hev1) video in Main and Main 10 (4:2:0 of 8 or 10 bits), with IDR, CRA and BLA pictures as keyframes and their RASL and RADL leading pictures (x265’s open groups included): an iPhone’s video, its HDR video’s HLG base layer included (its Dolby Vision metadata is recorded, not applied).

  • Motion JPEG tracks, one component or three, 8-bit baseline, extended or progressive: the sample entries jpeg, mjpa, dmb1, and mp4v as ffmpeg’s MP4 muxer writes it.

  • AAC-LC, HE-AAC, HE-AAC v2 and MP3 audio tracks, mono or stereo, in either container. An HE-AAC chunk’s high band is its own, not the whole stream’s: SBR’s noise runs on from frame to frame.

Not zarrified (listed with the reason, and kept in the mirror): AV1’s Professional and High profiles, Motion JPEG B and other video codecs, interlaced or 12-bit Motion JPEG, Dolby Vision’s own sample entries (dvh1, dvhe), avc3 (parameter sets in the stream), 4:2:2 and 4:4:4 H.264, 4:4:4 HEVC and VP9, groups of more than 1024 pictures, 24-bit PCM (bytes has no data type for it) and other audio codecs, timecode, text and metadata tracks.

Rejected: files without a moov box, files with none of the tracks above, and malformed boxes or sample tables.

Performance¶

  • Zarrifying reads the first 64 KiB, each top-level box header after it, and the moov box: for a file whose moov box is at the end, three or four requests. A fragmented file takes a request more per fragment (a recording cut into 2-second fragments, 1800 requests an hour).

  • Reading a video chunk is one request per run of its pictures in the file (the audio interleaved between them splits them) and decoding its pictures. A chunk starts at a keyframe, unless it splits a group longer than a chunk: then it also decodes the pictures back to the group’s keyframe. A smaller read costs the same: Zarr decodes whole chunks.

  • Decoding holds the chunk and the decoder’s reference pictures, never the lead-in’s pictures, and decodes Y, U and V once: reading U and V after Y takes the planes the decoding of Y kept.

  • The archive holds a reference per chunk, with a range per picture when the audio is interleaved, for each of the three planes, and about 4 bytes per frame of header: 2.4 MB for the 46 MB file above, two thirds of it the planes’ references, the same for y, u and v.

How it’s verified¶

js/test/mp4/verify.py zarrifies the synthetic MP4 and MOV files (including inputs to reject) with the browser code, reads every track through python/src/refkv, zarr-python and the refkv.stream codec, and compares it with ffmpeg’s decoding of the file (through PyAV, whose demuxer reads the tables itself): every plane of every picture, the presentation times and every audio sample, exactly; the HEVC files, 8- and 10-bit, in MP4 and QuickTime, are x265’s (fixtures/generators/mp4/write_fixtures.py). python/tests/ir/test_mp4.py does the same through Python, also with chunks of 7 frames, so that every track has several chunks and lead-ins. AAC coded with noise substitution is compared by its level, since a chunk decodes its noise bands from a generator of its own.
